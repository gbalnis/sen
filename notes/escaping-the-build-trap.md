# Reading Plan

## Goal

Use the book to participate credibly in product and leadership conversations, understand discovery and prioritization from a product manager's perspective, and assess whether your organization is optimizing for shipped work or meaningful results.

## Reconnaissance

Melissa Perri's central diagnosis is that organizations can mistake shipped features and busy roadmaps for value. Her proposed alternative connects customer problems to business value, makes strategy a guide for choices, uses discovery and experimentation to reduce uncertainty, and aligns organizational structures and incentives around outcomes.

This is a practitioner-oriented product management book, not a neutral survey of every product operating model. It should be especially useful if you work with teams that receive feature requests, delivery targets, or backlogs without clear success measures. The frameworks are most useful as shared language and hypotheses to test, not as a prescription to copy wholesale.

The author’s [Product Institute book guide](https://productinstitute.com/resources/books/escaping-the-build-trap) identifies five main chapters and summarizes the book's central ideas. The publisher's O'Reilly page was not accessible during preparation, so the chapter map below follows the author’s guide rather than a directly checked publisher table of contents. The [UXmatters review](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2021/08/book-review-escaping-the-build-trap.php) finds the advice useful but says the argument can feel repetitive; it also cautions that “product-led” may be misread as product management overruling engineering or user needs, and that some career-path advice may not fit every organization. A more favorable practitioner take appears in [Pondering the Product](https://ponderingtheproduct.com/book-review-escaping-the-build-trap/); treat its broad applicability claims as the reviewer's opinion, not proof.

## Vocabulary and Models

- **Output:** What a team produces or ships, such as a feature or release. It is activity, not evidence that the problem was solved.
- **Outcome:** A change in customer behavior or experience that creates value for customers and the business. Ask what changed, for whom, and how you know.
- **Value exchange:** The two-sided relationship in which customers receive value from a product and the business captures value in return.
- **Product strategy:** A decision framework that connects a product's direction to choices about where and how to create value. A roadmap is not strategy by itself.
- **Discovery:** Work to understand customer problems, test assumptions, and evaluate possible solutions before committing heavily to delivery.
- **Experiment:** A bounded test that produces evidence about an assumption or an approach. Prefer a learning goal and a success signal over an unqualified feature commitment.
- **Product Kata:** The improvement loop described in the author's guide: understand the direction, analyze the current state, set the next measurable goal, and experiment toward it.
- **Product-led organization:** An organization that optimizes for product outcomes, with strategy, incentives, budgets, and culture aligned around solving customer problems that also grow the business.
- **Feature factory / build trap:** A pattern in which success is measured by delivery volume and teams keep shipping without confirming customer or business value.

Use the vocabulary to ask better questions, not to label colleagues: “What outcome are we aiming for?”, “Which assumption does this test?”, and “What evidence would change our next decision?”

## Chapter Guide

### 1. The Build Trap

- **Purpose and fit:** Establishes the problem the book is trying to solve; directly supports your organizational diagnosis.
- **Main thesis:** An organization enters the build trap when it treats shipping as success and neglects whether products create value.
- **Frameworks:** Output versus outcome; value exchange between customer and business. Use these to distinguish delivery progress from evidence of impact. They do not by themselves tell you which outcome to choose.
- **Prediction:** You expect Perri to show how delivery pressure and feature-focused goals can make teams look productive while the underlying customer problem remains.
- **First principles (synthesis):** The key is not how much a team ships, but whether its work changes something valuable for customers and the business.
- **Application prompt:** Pick one recent feature request. What customer problem and desired behavior change was it meant to address? What evidence exists that the change occurred?
- **Recall test, answer without notes:** What were the three main ideas? What surprised you? What will you do differently?

- **Purpose and fit:** Explains the role and the archetypes that can distort it; useful for understanding the decisions and constraints a PM is responsible for.
- **Main thesis:** Product management is a value-creating role, not simply backlog administration or project coordination. The author's guide frames the chapter around the actual job and role archetypes that get it wrong.

### 3. Strategy

- **Purpose and fit:** Moves from diagnosing the trap to setting direction and making coherent choices; central to leadership conversations about priorities.
- **Main thesis:** Strategy should work as a decision framework, not as a static plan or a list of initiatives.
- **Frameworks:** Product strategy as decision-making guidance. The author's guide also connects real strategy to the value exchange and the organization's choices about where to create value. Record the book's specific strategy components and how they fit together.
- **Prediction:** You expect Perri to argue that strategy is useful when it helps teams decide what not to do and links product direction to customer and business value.
- **First principles (synthesis):** A strategy is only useful if it changes choices when new information or competing requests arrive.
- **Application prompt:** Can you describe your product's target customer, problem, desired outcome, and a meaningful choice the current strategy rules in or out?
- **Recall test, answer without notes:** What were the three main ideas? What surprised you? What will you do differently?

### 4. Product Management Process

- **Purpose and fit:** Connects strategic direction to discovery and delivery; most directly relevant to how product managers prioritize and experiment.
- **Main thesis:** Product work should be a repeatable learning process that moves from direction to understanding the current state, then toward a measurable next goal through experiments.
- **Frameworks:** Product Kata: understand the direction, analyze the current state, set the next measurable goal, and experiment toward it. The loop gives teams a way to make progress under uncertainty; it is not a guarantee that a given experiment or metric is right.
- **Prediction:** You expect the chapter to frame discovery as continuous learning and delivery as one possible response to evidence, not the default output of a fixed backlog.
- **First principles (synthesis):** Decide what you need to learn before deciding what to build; use evidence to choose the next step.
- **Application prompt:** For a current initiative, write down the direction, current evidence, next measurable target, riskiest assumption, and smallest useful experiment.
- **Recall test, answer without notes:** What were the three main ideas? What surprised you? What will you do differently?

### 5. The Product-Led Organization

- **Purpose and fit:** Extends product practice to the operating model and leadership system; most directly supports your goal of diagnosing and steering culture.
- **Main thesis:** A product-led organization aligns culture, structure, process, budgets, and incentives around product outcomes rather than shipping volume.
- **Frameworks:** Product-led organization as an organizational model; outcomes as the organizing logic. Use it to inspect whether authority and rewards support problem-solving. The label is not a reason to privilege product management over engineering, design, sales, or customer evidence.
- **Prediction:** You expect Perri to show that individual teams cannot escape the build trap if leadership goals, funding, and organizational structures keep rewarding output.
- **First principles (synthesis):** Culture follows what the organization funds, measures, and rewards, not only what its leaders say matters.
- **Application prompt:** Who gets praised or promoted for what? Which recurring review, planning, or funding mechanism rewards output, and what small change could make outcomes more visible?
- **Recall test, answer without notes:** What were the three main ideas? What surprised you? What will you do differently?

## Culture Diagnosis

Use these as evidence-gathering prompts, not as a scorecard to label the whole organization:

- Are teams given customer or business problems, or a list of features to ship?
- Do goals describe behavioral or business change, or completion and volume?
- Can teams change course when discovery contradicts the request?
- Are customer research, analytics, and experimentation available early enough to shape decisions?
- Do planning, funding, and performance reviews reinforce the outcomes leaders say they value?
- When a release misses its intended result, is the response learning and adjustment, or blame and another launch target?

Choose one leverage point, such as adding an outcome hypothesis to a roadmap item or reviewing a metric after launch. Start with a low-risk team-level experiment; seek leadership sponsorship if the change depends on incentives, funding, or decision rights. Keep delivery measures where they reveal flow or reliability, but don't treat them as customer value.

2. During the chapter, note the author's exact terms and models, plus one disagreement or assumption to test.
